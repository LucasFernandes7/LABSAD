\section{Critical Analysis, Gaps and Trends}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Comparison of the Approaches}
\label{sec:comparison}

The studies fall into five families according to the method at the core of the prioritisation: MCDM, risk and FMEA, hybrid approaches with ML, optimisation and scheduling, and simulation or industrial practice (Table~\ref{tab:families}, Appendix~\ref{app:figures}). None is better in every respect. MCDM and risk-based methods are transparent and work with expert knowledge alone, but they produce a static ranking that ignores resources unless coupled with optimisation. Optimisation handles resources and time but usually receives the priorities as given. Hybrid approaches use condition data but depend on large histories and add components the user cannot inspect. Studies grounded in industrial practice measure the effect on production but are specific to one line or plant and are hard to generalise.

\subsection{Gaps}
\label{sec:gaps}

Six gaps emerge from the analysis, each pointing to a requirement for a decision support system.

\textbf{G1. Safety is rarely a separate condition.} It is explicit in only seven studies and is usually aggregated with other factors~\cite{panwar2021,delgado2026}, so a low safety score can be offset elsewhere. A DSS should treat safety and regulatory requirements as non-compensable constraints.

\textbf{G2. Ranking and scheduling are separate.} Most MCDM and risk-based studies ignore crews, parts and downtime windows, while some scheduling models take priorities as given~\cite{gao2024}; few chain both~\cite{rios2025,danach2026,darko2026,moradi2024}. A DSS should derive priorities and plan interventions in the same process.

\textbf{G3. Priorities are rarely updated.} Only five studies revise priorities through a feedback loop or rolling horizon. A DSS should recompute them when new condition data, failures or work orders appear.

\textbf{G4. Weights lack a solid basis.} They are assumed equal, unreported or taken from few experts~\cite{delgado2026,wittbrodt2026,ozcan2021}, and sensitivity analysis is rare~\cite{bahrami2021,rios2025,wehbi2026,darko2026}. A DSS should make weights explicit, editable and tested for sensitivity.

\textbf{G5. Explanation and collaboration are limited.} Twenty studies explain their recommendations only partially or not at all, only one links each decision to its data~\cite{danach2026}, and no system supports a joint decision between maintenance, production and safety. A DSS should justify each priority by criterion and let several stakeholders review the plan.

\textbf{G6. Evidence of real impact is weak.} Only six studies applied their results in the plant, always without a control group. A DSS should record decisions and outcomes so that its effect can be measured over time.

\subsection{Trends}
\label{sec:trends}

Nine of the 31 studies date from 2026, including five of the seven hybrid ML studies, against one in 2021~\cite{astepe2021}. Four trends stand out: prediction feeding optimisation directly~\cite{danach2026,darko2026,wehbi2026,dui2025}; prescriptive systems that generate and rank alternative actions~\cite{wittbrodt2026}; explainability through SHAP values or decomposed risk scores~\cite{danach2026,tang2026}; and digital twins and IoT architectures as the data layer~\cite{dui2025,dui2026}, together with learning-based scheduling~\cite{li2027}. The most recent proposals are also the most complex, and none has yet been evaluated over a long period of real operation.
